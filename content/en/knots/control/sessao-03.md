# SESSION 3, THE CONTROL: HOW IT WORKS IN YOU

**Tier:** 1 or 2 · **Access:** 3 days after Session 2

## Block A, The Mechanics

Your control isn't cruelty. It isn't arrogance. It's the only way you know to feel safe.

It works like this: you don't trust that things will resolve without you. You learned, long ago, that if you're not managing, something will fail, and that failure will land on you. So you manage everything. The house. The children. His schedule. The holidays. What he wears to your mother's event. Who sends the message to his father on his birthday. What gift to buy for his mother. Which school to choose. Which car to buy. When to change the sheets.

You know it seems like a lot. You know you seem like a machine. You know he sometimes complains, "fine, fine, you do it," and then he walks away, and the silence between you at dinner carries the weight of someone who gave up.

But you also know that if you let go for a single day, the chaos returns. You've tested it. You've tried "letting him handle it." The result was always the same: he handled it badly, or late, or forgot, or did a version of what you'd do but worse.

So you went back to doing it.

And he, after years of this, stopped proposing. Stopped deciding. Stopped showing up with ideas. He learned, without ever saying it aloud, that it's better to stay quiet and let you handle it than to try and be corrected. In his eyes, you didn't ask for help; you demanded perfection. And perfection is tiring to have demanded of you.

What you feel as "he turned into a child" is the consequence of having become the manager he never asked for.

Notice the signs of control acting in you:

You correct what he does, even when he does it well.

You give him instructions for tasks he's done a hundred times.

You can't see something out of place without fixing it, even if he put it there five minutes ago.

When he decides something without consulting you, you feel disrespected, ignored, or both.

When he asks what you want for dinner, you answer "whatever you want," then feel upset by his choice.

You've given up asking and prefer to do it yourself, even if it exhausts you.

There are parts of the house where you know where every item is, and he can't find anything.

This isn't bad. It's an old system trying to keep you safe in a world you learned, as a child, not to trust.

## Block B, The Secret Language

Control speaks in you in phrases that sound sensible. Each one is the knot justifying itself.

> *"If it's not me, it won't get done."*

> *"I'm not controlling, I'm just organizing."*

> *"He knows I know better. That's why he doesn't decide."*

> *"If I weren't paying attention, all of this would fall apart."*

> *"It's not that I'm intense, it's that he's careless."*

> *"I'm just explaining how it's done."*

> *"I'm not scolding, I'm correcting."*

> *"Let him do it his way, then he can clean up his own mess."*

> *"I'd also love not to have to think about everything. But I have no choice."*

Each of these has a foot in truth. That's what makes them dangerous. Control never lies completely. It mixes a small truth with an old need, and you believe you're being rational.

You'll notice, as we go on, that some of these phrases became your voice. You can no longer tell the difference between "me" and "my control." That's normal. You haven't dissolved it yet; you're only beginning to see.

## Block C, Reflection

Write. Not for me. For yourself.

**Question:** In which specific area does your control have the most power? At home? With the children? With his work? With the extended family? With money? Describe, in 5-10 lines, a concrete situation from the last 7 days where you matched this description. Don't soften it.

## Block D, Practice for the Next 3 Days

**Don't Correct.**

For 3 days, you'll choose **one thing** that your husband normally does "wrong," by your standard, and you won't correct it.

It could be:
- The way he unloads the dishes
- The way he dresses the kids to go out
- How he folds the towels
- How he drives
- The way he replies to family messages
- What he tells them when you're not there

Choose one. **Just one.** More than that is too much to start with.

For 3 days, when it happens, you'll:

1. **Say nothing.** Not with words. Not with tone. Not with a look.
2. **Don't correct after.** Don't reorganize when he leaves the room. Don't redo what he did.
3. **Notice what you feel.** You'll get an inner itch. Physical anxiety will appear. You'll feel something is wrong with the world. Notice that sensation without acting on it.

It isn't easy. Control will scream in you that this will go wrong, that the house will collapse, that he'll build the wrong habit forever. **None of that is true.** It's 3 days. The house can handle it. Your nervous system is the one in panic, because it's used to feeling useful by correcting.

Each night, write one line: *Today, when he _____, I didn't correct. What I felt was _____.*

In 3 days, Session 4 opens. We're going to descend to the origin of this control.
