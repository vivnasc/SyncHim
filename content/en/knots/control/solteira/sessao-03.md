# SESSION 3, THE CONTROL: HOW IT WORKS IN YOU (single)

**Tier:** 1 or 2 | **Knot:** Control | **Context:** single | **Access:** 3 days after Session 2

---

## Block A, The Mechanics

Your control isn't "having standards." It isn't "knowing what you want." It's the only way you know to feel safe, and controlling who enters your life is your way of making sure no one catches you off guard.

It works like this: you evaluate everyone. Before the second date, you already have a list of what's wrong with him. The way he texts. The restaurant he chose. The fact he arrived three minutes late, or too early, or ordered the wrong thing. You notice everything, and every detail becomes a criterion for elimination.

You call this "not wasting time on the wrong ones." And in part you're right: you deserve standards. But there's a difference between standards and the impossible sieve you use. Standards know what matters. Your sieve eliminates people for things that don't matter, because the secret goal isn't to find someone. It's to keep control.

Because as long as you evaluate, you're in charge. As long as you filter, you're safe. The moment you let yourself like someone without reservations, you lose control, and losing control is exactly what your system fears most.

So you sabotage in subtle ways. You test men (and they fail tests they didn't know existed). You create rules (what time he should call, when he should propose the next date, how he should behave). And when they don't follow rules they never knew about, you disqualify them with the feeling of having "dodged another one."

And men sense the exam. Nobody relaxes under constant evaluation. They pull away, or you push them first, and you're left feeling that "there are no men good enough." Some probably weren't. But several were, and they never got close enough to show you.

Notice the signs of control acting in you:

You eliminate men over small details before getting to know them.

You have unspoken rules about how he "should" behave, and feel disappointed when he can't guess them.

You test men without telling them they're being tested.

You feel discomfort when you like someone to the point of "losing your head."

You'd rather be the one to end it than sit with the uncertainty of not controlling what happens.

When someone pleases you too much, you quickly find a reason to doubt or lose interest.

You rarely let yourself be swept away. You're always managing the impression, the rhythm, the next step.

This isn't a flaw. It's an old system that learned that controlling is the only way not to be hurt. But it's the same system preventing you from having what you say you want.

## Block B, The Secret Language

Control speaks in you in phrases that sound like maturity, self-knowledge, healthy standards. Each one is the knot in disguise.

> *"I just know what I want."*

> *"I won't lower my standards."*

> *"If he really liked me, he'd do X."*

> *"I can tell right away when it won't work."*

> *"I don't have time for games."*

> *"I'd rather be alone than badly accompanied."*

> *"I test to see if he's serious."*

> *"I'm demanding because I value myself."*

Each has a foot in truth. Yes, standards matter. Yes, self-respect matters. That's what makes them poisonous. Control never lies completely. It mixes legitimate self-respect with an old need to control everything, and you believe you're being discerning when you're being afraid.

The difference between standards and control is simple: standards open space for the right person to appear and show themselves. Control closes the space before anyone gets a chance. Standards say "I deserve good." Control says "I can't risk."

## Block C, Reflection

Write. Not for me. For yourself.

**Question:** Think of the last man you disqualified early. What was the "reason"? And be honest: was that reason truly about real incompatibility, or was it a pretext for not having to risk liking him? Write, in 5-10 lines, about that situation. Don't soften it.

## Block D, Practice for the Next 3 Days

**Don't Eliminate on First Impression.**

For 3 days, with any man you interact with (on an app, on a date, someone who approaches you), you'll practise **not eliminating over the first flaw**.

When the impulse to disqualify appears, *"he texts badly," "he took too long to reply," "he picked a bad place," "not my type"*, stop. Notice the impulse. And ask yourself: *is this truly incompatibility, or is it my control looking for an exit?*

You don't have to continue with anyone you don't want. The practice isn't forcing interest. It's **not cutting automatically**. Giving one more date. One more conversation. Letting the person show themselves before you sieve them out.

If there's no one right now, the practice becomes observation: notice how many times, during the day, you eliminate, judge, or discard, people, situations, possibilities, over the first sign of imperfection.

You'll feel discomfort. Control will scream that you're "lowering standards," "wasting time," "ignoring red flags." Learn to distinguish a real red flag (disrespect, lying, aggression) from a control excuse (spelling error, dressed badly, laughed too loudly).

Each night, write: *Today I wanted to eliminate _____ times. In _____ of them it was control, not real incompatibility.*
