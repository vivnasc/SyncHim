# SESSION 4 — THE HUNGER: THE ORIGIN

**Tier:** 1 or 2 · **Access:** 3 days after Session 3

## Block A — How Hunger Is Born

Before reading further, stay with this: your hunger wasn't born in your marriage.

When hunger began in you, you didn't have a husband. You weren't old enough to have a husband. You were maybe five. Maybe seven. Maybe younger.

Hunger is always born in childhood, in women who grew up looking at someone who didn't look back — or who looked back unpredictably.

It might have been an absent mother. Depressed. Too busy surviving to see you.

A mother present in body but distant in soul — always in her head, always tired, always doing something else when you needed her.

A mother excessively focused on a brother, husband, illness, career, drama.

An absent father — physically or emotionally — whose attention you learned to seek with whatever behaviour worked.

A family where affection was earned: you had to be a good student, good daughter, good sister, quiet, helpful. Those who did everything right received care as payment.

It doesn't matter which story is yours. The result was the same: you learned, very early, that the other's presence is not guaranteed. It's something you earn, lose, win, plead for. You didn't grow up knowing you'd be loved simply for existing. You grew up knowing you'd have to **be a certain way** to be loved.

Adult hunger is the continuation of that learning. Your husband is not your father, not your mother — but the inner system you built to survive that childhood keeps operating on him.

That's why, when he pulls away a little, you don't just miss him. You feel the old missing. The lonely bedroom. The silent dinner table. The lap that never came.

## Block B — The Descent to the Root

Write slowly. Don't answer fast. Each question is a descent. If you feel resistance, you're close.

**1.** As a child, who did you most want to look at you? Did they?

[ Writing space ]

**2.** Do you remember a specific moment — concrete, with place and scene — when you asked for attention and were told to be quiet, ignored, or treated as if you were exaggerating?

[ Writing space ]

**3.** Did your mother know what you were feeling, or did you know better what she was feeling than she knew what you were?

[ Writing space ]

**4.** Before your husband, with whom else did you feel this hunger? A boyfriend? A friend? A boss? How long until you recognised it was the same sensation?

[ Writing space ]

**5.** If you didn't need him so much, who would you be?

[ Writing space ]

## Block C — What You Just Touched

What you just touched has a name. It's called the **wound of the unseen**.

It isn't trauma in the dramatic sense. Not abuse. Not catastrophe. Something subtler and more common: the childhood experience of not being seen by those who should have seen you.

Women with this wound develop an extraordinary ability to read others — because they had to learn, as children, to read imperceptible signals to decide if it was safe to ask for closeness or better to stay quiet. This capacity is real. It's one of your actual gifts. You read your husband better than he reads himself.

But the same capacity that made you skilled at reading others made you unable to see yourself. You looked outward your whole life. You **are** your attention given to others. Removing attention from the other feels like disappearing.

It isn't. But it feels that way.

The good news — and there is good news — is that wounds of the unseen do dissolve. Not with endless therapy. Not with mirror affirmations. They dissolve when you, slowly, over months, begin doing something new: **looking at yourself with the same attention you've always given others.**

That's what we'll begin to practise now.

## Block D — Practice for the Next 3 Days

**Five Minutes of You.**

For 3 days, reserve five minutes per day (five — not more) just to ask yourself: *what am I feeling right now?*

Sit. Phone out of reach. Eyes closed. Stay with yourself.

You'll notice three things:

**First:** the immediate urge to think about him. About something of his. About something between you. Hunger will pull you outward. Come back. Ask again: *me, right now, feeling what?*

**Second:** you'll realise you don't know. The answer won't come. You're not used to looking inward with this kind of question. That's normal. Persist without forcing.

**Third:** if you stay 5 minutes without fleeing, something will appear. Could be tiredness. A small anger. Empty sadness. Boredom. A wish you didn't know you had. Whatever appears, stay with it. You don't have to do anything with it.

End of each day, write one line: *Today, during the 5 minutes, I felt _____.*

If nothing came, write "nothing." That's information.
